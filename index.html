SVMs, visually

Of all the lines that separate two classes, take the one farthest from every sample.

How far is a sample from the line?

Goal: the SVM picks the line farthest from every sample, so first we need the distance, or margin, from a point x to the line w·x = b.

Approach: six steps, from the line through the origin to any point (the panel beside the plot). Each step leans on the one before.

margin(x) = w·x − b‖w‖ ⇒ with ‖w‖ = 1:  x·w − b

Labels are now y ∈ {−1, +1} and ŷ = sign(w·x − b). A sample is on its own class's side exactly when y(w·x − b) > 0.

Hard margin: the widest street

Goal: many lines separate the classes. Pick the one whose nearest sample is farthest away.

Approach: rescale w and b so the nearest sample has y(w·x − b) = 1. The margin is then 1/‖w‖, so the widest street has the shortest w (Boser, Guyon & Vapnik 1992):

min 12 ‖w‖2  subject to  yi(w·xi − b) ≥ 1 for every i

With Lagrange multipliers (lesson 15): w = ∑ αiyixi and ∑ αiyi = 0, with αi ≥ 0, and αi = 0 unless the constraint is active, yi(w·xi − b) = 1. The samples with active constraints sit on the street's edge: the support vectors.

Soft margin: pay for slack

Goal: when the classes overlap, no line meets every constraint. Let sample i fall short by its slack ξi ≥ 0, at a price of C per unit (Cortes & Vapnik 1995).

min 12 ‖w‖2 + C ∑ ξi  subject to  yi(w·xi − b) ≥ 1 − ξi

Hinge view: at the best w, b, each slack is as small as its constraint allows,

ξi = max(0, 1 − yi(w·xi − b))

so the SVM is hinge loss plus a ‖w‖² penalty: the “loss + penalty” shape of ridge (lesson 13), with C playing 1/λ. A stick runs from each sample with slack to its own class's margin line; its length is ξi/‖w‖.

Try this

  • In step 1, drag the tip of w to make it twice as long. Does the line change?
    AnswerNo. Only the direction of w sets the line w·x = 0: w and 2w give the same points. Step 3 makes this the reason we may assume ‖w‖ = 1.
  • In step 2, point w straight up, at [0, 1]. Which line is it?
    Answerx₁ = 0, the horizontal axis: slope −0/1 = 0. Every vector along it is [t, 0], and [0, 1]·[t, 0] = 0.
  • In step 6, drag x parallel to the line, then across it. When does the margin change, and when is it negative?
    AnswerMoving parallel to the line leaves x·w (and the margin) alone; only motion along w changes it. It is 0 on the line and negative on the side w points away from.
  • Drag the square handles (or empty space, to slide the line) to make your margin as large as you can. Then check Show widest line.
  • Drag a sample that is not a support vector, staying off the violet street. Does the widest line move?
    AnswerNo. Its constraint is inactive, so its α = 0 and it is not in w = ∑ αiyixi. Only the samples on the street's edge shape the line.
  • Drag a support vector toward the other class.
    AnswerThe street narrows: ‖w‖ grows, so 1/‖w‖ shrinks, and the αs grow. Past the other class's samples there is no separating line at all.
  • In the w = ∑ αiyixi card, why are there support vectors from both classes?
    Answer∑ αiyi = 0 with every αi > 0 needs some yi = +1 and some −1. Geometrically: the street has an edge on each side.
  • Slide C from 0.01 to 1000. What happens to the margin and the number of support vectors? Compare the chart under the hinge.
    AnswerSmall C: slack is cheap, so a wide street with many samples inside it (all support vectors). Large C: slack is expensive, so the street narrows to push samples out, and fewer support vectors remain.
  • On the hinge chart, which samples are support vectors?
    AnswerEvery sample with y(w·x − b) ≤ 1: at the hinge's corner (on the margin, 0 < α < C) or on its slope (slack > 0, α = C, dashed rings). Samples on the flat part have α = 0.
  • Set Separation to 4 and C to 1000. Is there any slack left?
    AnswerNo: the classes separate, every ξi = 0, and the fit is the hard margin. A large C approaches the hard margin; with overlap the hard margin does not exist.

Drag the white points and the tip of w. From step 3 on, drag the line itself to change b.

1. The line w·x = 0

In 2-d, w·x = w₀x₀ + w₁x₁ = 0 solves to

x₁ = −w₀w₁ x₀

a line through the origin with slope −w₀ / w₁ (the green triangle).

2. w is orthogonal to the line

Take any two points xA, xB on the line. Both satisfy w·x = 0, so

w·(xA − xB) = 0 − 0 = 0

w is orthogonal to every vector along the line. This is lesson 15's “∇g is orthogonal to its level set”, with g(x) = w·x and ∇g = w.

3. Assume ‖w‖ = 1

Let the line be w·x = b (drag it to change b). For any k > 0, (kw)·x = kb holds at exactly the same points. Slide ‖w‖: the arrow changes, the line does not. So we may as well take ‖w‖ = 1, the dashed unit circle.

4. The point bw is on the line

With ‖w‖ = 1,

w·(bw) = b‖w‖2 = b

so bw is on the line, where the w axis meets it at a right angle (step 2). It is the line's closest point to the origin, at distance b.

5. The margin of a point on the w axis

The point x = αw lies on the w axis, which crosses the line at bw at a right angle. So its distance to the line is

‖αw − bw‖ = |α − b| ‖w‖ = |α − b|

Keep the sign: the margin α − b is positive on the side w points to. Drag αw.

6. The margin of any point

Project x onto the w axis: the foot is cw with

c = x·ww·w = x·w ⇒ margin = x·w − b

Sliding x parallel to the line keeps its distance, so x and cw have the same margin: step 5 with α = c. The two green segments match. For any length of w, the margin is (w·x − b) / ‖w‖.
